 Abstract
 Much previous research about fracture of deformable bodies has
 focused on physical principles (e.g. energy and mass conservation),
 leading to simulation methods that are very realistic, but not yet
 applicable in real-time. We present a stylized animation method
 for destruction that is visually plausible and capable of running at
 hundreds of frames per second by sacrificing visual realism and
 physical accuracy.
 In this paper we describe a new volume-preserving voxel con
straint based on Gram-Schmidt orthonomalization for real-time
 animation of soft bodies. This constraint can be used in tandemwith
 a breakable face-to-face voxel constraint to create destructible mod
els. The model creation pipeline and constraints presented here are
 designed to minimize the number of partitions needed for parallel
 Gauss-Seidel iterations. We compare the proposed techniques with
 shape constraints and the state-of-the-art material point method
 on the basis of memory usage, computation time and visual results.

 1 Introduction
 Modern graphics hardware and APIs have enabled not only high
 visual fidelity in real-time graphics, but also high-performance
 animation and simulation. The parallel processing capability of
 the GPU is exposed to developers through dedicated APIs, such
 as OpenCL and CUDA, and through compute shader support in
 graphics APIs, such as Vulkan, DirectX, OpenGL, and WebGPU.
 However, accurate physical simulation of render-quality assets,
 which can have hundreds of thousands of vertices is still not feasi
ble in real-time applications, such as games, that must divide their
 frame budget among many other tasks. This problem can be over
come by embedding the rest pose of a high-resolution mesh in a
 lower-resolution control surface, and transferring its deformations
 back to the mesh. Handling fracture and destruction within this
 framework is achallenge which must beovercomeforthefull range
 of material behaviors to be realized in real-time applications.
 Simple building-blocks, such as tetrahedra [Wojtan and Turk
 2008] and hexahedra [Muller et al. 2004] are commonly used be
cause they have few vertices and their geometric properties are
 generally easier to compute than those of more complex shapes.
 In this work we consider deformable voxels which are initialized
 as a voxelization of an input mesh. Although it may be possible to
 apply Delaunay tetrahedralization to many triangle meshes, the
 resulting mesh will often have some undesirable features, such as
 narrow tetrahedra and high-degree vertices. Narrow tetrahedra can
 result in numerical issues when computing volume and project
ing constraints. Variable tetrahedron size also makes it difficult to

 McGraw
 use particles as collision primitives in particle-based frameworks
 since the tetrahedron cannot be effectively covered by particles
 of the same radius positioned at its vertices. High-degree vertices
 pose a challenge to parallelization since vertex degree is a lower
 limit on the number of partitions required when using Gauss-Seidel
 iterations to parallelize constraint projection. The purpose of par
titioning is to group constraints into sets such that no particle is
 affected by more than one constraint in the set, which avoids par
allel read/write conflicts. Minimizing the number of partitions is
 important for real-time applications because it minimizes the num
ber of compute shader dispatches and memory barriers we must
 execute per frame. Jacobi iteration, an alternative to Gauss-Seidel
 iteration, does not require partitioning, but generally leads to slow
 convergence and less stiff results [Bender et al. 2017] and relies on
 slow atomic operations to avoid read/write conflicts.
 The voxel grid structure is regular and the maximum vertex de
gree is fixed at 8. We can easily partition voxel constraints without
 solving a graph-coloring [Abu Rumman and Fratarcangeli 2015]
 or clustering [Ton-That et al. 2023] problem. A cubical voxel can
 be covered with particles of the same radius without large gaps
 between particles, allowing simple particle-based collision detec
tion. However, a challenge with deformable voxels is that the 8
 vertices initially forming a cube may not remain a cube or even a
 parallelepiped when vertices are allowed to move independently.
 In fact, the faces may not even remain planar. The volume of such
 a general shape is difficult to formulate, making constraining the
 volume with gradient-based techniques challenging. Macklin and
 Müller et al. [Macklin and Muller 2021] overcome this problem by
 approximating the cube with 5 tetrahedra. In parallel implementa
tion, the 5 tetrahedral constraints can be solved sequentially within
 a single thread, allowing the partitioning benefits of the regular
 voxel structure to be maintained.
 In this work we describe our system which was previously
 demonstrated [McGraw 2024] at SIGGRAPH Real-Time Live. We
 take advantage of the simple voxel geometry to define shape and
 connectivity constraints, and we exploit the regularity of the voxel
 grid to optimize our parallel implementation by minimizing the
 numberofconstraint partitions. Our constraints are based on Gram
Schmidt orthogonalization, and have several important benefits
 over alternative methods. Our constraint is a soft constraint with
 relaxation parameters that permits the 8 voxel vertices to approx
imate a parallelpiped after volume-preserving deformation. Our
 constraint does not suffer from the bias problem that is inherent to
 other variants of Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization, and requires
 less memory than previously proposed shape constraints. We also
 describe a new breakable face constraint that permits us to explore
 a range of inhomogeneous and anisotropic materials in real-time
 destruction, only requiring a total of 4 constraint partitions, making
 parallel computation fast.
 2 Related work
 Stable and fast simulation and physically-based animation of de
formable objects has been a goal of graphics research for decades.
 Many approaches and representations have been proposed, such as
 f
 inite difference optimization of parametric surfaces [Terzopoulos
 et al. 1987], mass-spring systems [Desbrun et al. 1999], and finite
 element methods [Wojtan and Turk 2008].
 Jakobsen [Jakobsen 2001] described the real-time techniques
 used to animate physically-based models built from systems of
 constrained particles in the game Hitman. His approach worked in
 terms of particle positions, without explicitly keeping track of linear
 or angular accelerations. This work was a forerunner to position
based dynamics (PBD) approaches, and was followed by many
 variations and extensions, most importantly, general techniques for
 formulating and solving different types of constraints [Macklin et al.
 2014; Müller et al. 2007]. Bender et al. [Bender et al. 2017] and Fang
 et al. [Fang et al. 2023] have written surveys which cover much of
 the related work in the 20 years following Jakobsen’s paper.
 Acompelling feature of PBD systems is that plausible rotational
 behavior emerges as a side effect of constraints between particles.
 This permits large-scale models built from constrained particles to
 behave naturally, without having to explicitly compute physical
 properties, such as the moment of inertia. Although a surprising
 array of complex structures can be built from particles and distance
 constraints, volumetric elements make it convenient to accurately
 model more complex phenomena.
 Tetrahedra seem to be a natural choice of volume element for
 physically-based animation of triangle meshes. However, Macklin
 and Müller observed an issue with tetrahedral meshes [Macklin and
 Muller 2021], namely that parallelization is hindered by the large
 number of partitions required to avoid read/write conflicts. They
 proposed instead a regular hexahedral grid which requires only
 8 partitions. Each hexahedron is filled with 5 tetrahedral volume
 constraints which can be solved by serializing them within a single
 thread, permitting the volume of the hexahedral element to be
 constrained.
 Shape matching constraints are a geometric, but physically
plausible approach to animating rigid shapes. Imposing the con
straint involves finding the optimal rotation and translations that
 align the rest pose shape and the deformed shape. The translations
 (pre- and post-rotation) can be written in terms of the rest pose and
 deformed shape centroids. It is more difficult to find the optimal
 rotation, so the problem is solved by instead finding the optimal
 linear transformation matrix (denoted 𝐴𝑝𝑞 in [Müller et al. 2005]),
 then factoring out the rotational part of that matrix using singular
 value decomposition (SVD) or polar decomposition.
 The shape-matching technique has been adopted in many appli
cations. Shape matching constraints were combined with oriented
 particles (ellipsoids) [Müller and Chentanez 2011] to create sparse
 models for complex meshes. Jones et al. [Jones et al. 2016] com
pute ductile fracture in terms of dense clusters of particles with
 local shape matching constraints. Physically-based shape matching
 using Hookean and Neo-Hookean models were later described by
 Müller at al. [Müller et al. 2022].
 In this work, we propose the use of Gram-Schmidt (GS) orthono
malization for voxel shape constraints. GS approaches were iden
tified as being potentially useful in shape matching [Müller et al.
 2016], but other approaches, such as polar decomposition were
 preferred due to the bias problem inherent to GS. Gram-Schmidt
 orthogonalization takes a set of linearly independent vectors and
 generates a set of orthonormal vectors. There are two variations
 of the algorithm. Both classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS) and modified

 Gram-Schmidt (MGS) are biased in the sense that the results de
pend on the processing order of the vectors. In infinite precision
 arithmetic they would give the same result, but in finite precision
 the MGS computation is preferred since it is less sensitive to the
 accumulation of round-off errors [Golub and Van Loan 2013]. In
 this work we present a shape constraint based on a variation of GS
 which mitigates the bias problem.
 Compared to traditional shape constraints, our proposed ap
proach has several benefits. Since the rest shape of the deformable
 voxel is a cube centered at the origin, we do not need to store the
 rest shape explicitly. We constrain the voxel using a GS-like process
 applied to voxel edges, so the quaternion or rotation matrix used to
 “warm-start” the polar decomposition [Müller et al. 2016] does not
 need to be stored. These two benefits mean that for our destructible
 models, the voxel shape constraints require no additional memory,
 and other constraints based on this technique, like the face-to-face
 voxel constraints proposed in this paper, occupy very little memory.
 Additionally, our constraint is a volume-preserving soft constraint
 where the degree of softness can be controlled with two relaxation
 parameters, so that the solution can be a parallelepiped instead of a
 cube, resulting in a more natural soft-body behavior. By constrast,
 shape-matching computes a rigid transformation between rest pose
 and deformed pose.
 Techniques for fracture have been proposed since the earliest
 days of deformable models. Terzopolous and Fleicher [Terzopoulos
 and Fleischer 1988] implemented fracture in surfaces defined as
 piecewise continuous polynomial splines by breaking continuity
 between elements. O’Brien fractured FEM models [O’Brien et al.
 2002; O’Brien and Hodgins 1999] by splitting tetrahedral elements.
 In extended finite element methods (XFEM), such as the one de
scribed by Koshier et al. [Koschier et al. 2017], cutting without
 domain remeshing is performed by introducing discontinuous local
 enrichment functions to the finite element shape functions.
 Recently, there has been renewed interest in hybrid Eulerian
Lagragian techniques, like material point methods (MPM) [Jiang
 et al. 2016]. Like the particle-in-cell (PIC) methods, such as FLIP
 for incompressible fluid simulation [Zhu and Bridson 2005], these
 methods use both Lagrangian particles and an Eulerian grid. A wide
 variety of materials may be simulated, including fluids, anisotropic
 solids [Mandal et al. 2022], granular materials [Stomakhin et al.
 2013] and interacting solid/fluid systems [Yan et al. 2018]. Simula
tion proceeds by updating particle state and scattering values to
 the grid, iterating the grid values, and then gathering values back
 to particles from the grid. These methods are capable of impressive
 results, including change of topology (fracture), but real-time im
plementation is challenging due to the high memory bandwidth
 required by particle-to-grid and grid-to-particle memory transfers.
 Since it is a meshless method, rendering the fractured mesh requires
 additional work. Some approaches utilize the simulation grid (or a
 higher resolution grid) in volume rendering [Stomakhin et al. 2013],
 or to generate an isosurface [Wolper et al. 2019]. Other approaches
 based on the particles include tetrahedralizing the particles [Wang
 et al. 2019]. Our approach, since we are targeting a stylized voxel
 rendering, uses only the original input mesh and intersections of
 the mesh with a grid of voxels. The voxelized approach we propose
 is similar to previous approaches which use hexahedral elements
 linked together by their faces [Dick et al. 2010; Frisken-Gibson
 1999] and perform cutting by disconnecting links. Since our goal is
 a voxel-style real-time animation technique and not an engineering
 simulation, we do not perform any additional domain refinement
 or element splitting.
 3 Methods
 In this section we describe our new voxel shape constraint based
 on GS, and describe how we use it to create destructible soft body
 models in a PBD framework. Then we give an overview of the
 rendering process, which involves using task and mesh shaders to
 render the surface mesh and internal details computed from the
 voxelization.

 3.2 Voxelized model construction
 Webegin byvoxelizing the input mesh once at startup. Destructible
 models use a solid voxelization since interior voxels will be exposed
 during destruction, which means the input must be a closed, water
tight mesh. The voxelization technique used in our system [Crassin
 and Green 2012] uses the rendering pipeline to select the dominant
 triangle axis and perform conservative rasterization to voxelize
 triangles directly into a 3d texture. From the filled voxelization, we
 compute a distance transform volume [Strutz 2021] and a skeleton
 based on the medial axis [Lee et al. 1994]. We use these later to as
sign strain limits which determine when neighboring voxels break
 apart, and also to synthesize internal texture details.

 be tessellated so that triangles are not excessively large relative to

 An internal cube meshlet is also created for each voxel. These

 planes and clip the voxel plane geometry for each face to the voxel
 boundary when rendering internal voxel surfaces.
 We procedurally create internal texture details based on the
 distance transform of the voxel grid which is stored in a 3d texture.
 In the results shown in this paper, we display isocontours of the
 distance transform on the exposed surfaces using tricubic texture
 interpolation [Ruijters et al. 2008] due to the low resolution of the
 distance transform. The voxel skeleton could also be used to provide
 additional internal visual details, but currently we only use it for
 structural purposes. In production, a custom 3d texture could be
 authored for each destructible mesh.

 5 Conclusions
 Wehave described a new voxel shape constraint based on Gram
Schmidt orthonormalization, and applied it to real-time destruc
tible models computed from triangle meshes. The voxel constraint
 projects sets of 8 vertices onto a parallelepiped while avoiding the
 bias problem inherent in traditional Gram-Schmidt approaches. We
 have detailed the creation and simulation pipeline, and showed
 that the new voxel constraint is as fast as shape constraints and
 does not require additional matrix/quaternion storage. The VGS
 relaxation parameters give our method additional flexibility com
pared to shape constraints which rigidly map the rest pose to the
MIG’24, November 21–23, 2024, Arlington, VA, USA
 McGraw
 deformed state. Voxel shape constraints can be dispatched in a
 single partition.
 Weuse the VGS approach to create breakable face-to-face voxel
 constraints which glue voxels together until a predefined strain
 limit is exceeded. Since these constraints are applied on a face-by
face basis we can achieve a number of different anisotropic material
 destruction effects in real-time. Face-to-face constraints occupy
 only 8 bytes each and require only 3 partitions for Gauss-Seidel
 iterations, making parallel implementations fast. Though not a
 physically accurate simulation technique, we feel the results are
 plausible enough to be useful in real-time applications like games.
 In future work, we plan to implement new animation and render
ing features. Synthesizing smooth internal details for destructible
 meshes will allow them to be used in applications beyond voxel
 art style games. To increase the model detail we are investigating
 multigrid approaches that would permit us to animate regions of
 higher voxel resolution without sacrificing speed. To improve the
 stiffness of larger model, we will explore the use of voxel-based
 long-range constraints [Müller et al. 2017].
